	- Origin of thoughts
		- At this moment in human post-evolution (? #explore), we seem particularly preoccupied with tracing the origin of thoughts in terms of "giving credit where it's due", in the form of e.g. citations/references in academia, or "name-dropping" in general conversation minds/individuals we believe ideas emerged from. In some cases, this referencing (wherever it is along the formal-informal communicative spectrum) is non-egoic/self-transcendent in that its purpose is solely (or at least primarily) to provide context around the thought/idea we are talking about, in mentioning its (believed) thinker/originator (for example, it can be useful to understand in what historical or personal context a particular thought emerged). However, in many cases, this referencing is exactly the opposite, serving our ego in a kind of intellectual virtue signalling of demonstrating the extent of our knowledge for the sake of appearing knowledgeable. Often, this serves an important purpose of increasing our credibility (by e.g. appealing to the credibility of the individuals we are referencing, or by showing a general expertise to reassure inputters we know what we're talking about), and this is important (right now*) for "being taken seriously" when interthinking/communicating. In this way, referencing origins/individuals can be both (1) egoic and (2) non-egoic: (1) in acting to increase credibility of / respect toward self, but (2) in doing this for the broader goal of reaching more minds more effectively.
		- \*I say "right now" because perhaps in future we can transition to an era/approach to interthinking where more weight is assigned to raw thoughts themselves, independent of thinker. But at this time, the cultivation of credibility as a thinker is generally necessary for minimising bias when others input your thoughts.
		- But the point I intended to make before launching into this ramble is that, when we feel a need to store information about thought origins (e.g. individuals we encountered the thoughts from) in order to acknowledge their (lack of) originality, it creates a bogged-down-ness in our thinking - at least, this is how I feel; when I encounter a thought I find really important/useful and want to remember, and I'm overly preoccupied with remembering where/when/why the thought emerged (i.e., which mind outputted it originally (as far as I'm aware)), I find it harder to remember the important thought itself. So, it always feels like a waste of mental energy/exertion to try to recall who said what - I'd rather just recall the "what", so that I have more space for more "whats", rather than "who said whats".
		- And I feel this approach to inputting, processing and outputting thoughts (e.g. I hear/see mind X output thought Y, and I remember and process Y, and later output Y in a slightly altered form) should be acceptable, because I'm not making any claim of thought originality.
		- This is a reason I think it's valuable to categorise thoughts as emergent or encountered [[em-enc]].
