I'm trying to develop a taxonomy/framing through which to better conceive of the economy. I want to extend the notions of "production" and "consumption" to **provision** and **receipt**. I define the **basics** (B) as anything necessary for what a prominent majority would consider a decent standard of living, and everything beyond this as **beyond basics** (BB) and equivalently, **non-basics**. I define **required receipt** as the basics, and **desired-not-required receipt** as the non-basics, and the union of these as **desired receipt** (R). I define **required provision** as the provision necessary to provide the basics (required receipt), and **requested-not-required provision** as the provision necessary to provide the non-basics (desired-not-required receipt), and the union of these as **requested provision** (P). I define **desired provision** to be the union of all things we as individuals wish to provide, independent of whether anyone wants to receive them. So, the aim is to distinguish between what provision is necessary for the things we want to receive, and what provision we want to participate in for the sake of it. The aim is to create terminology to better pin-point current issues with the economy, such as the fact that there is a lot of *unnecessary provision* (unrequired, unrequested and undesired) and a lot of *unwanted receipt* (unrequired and undesired) - for example, abundance of stuff no one asked for, but we take it because it's available, overproduction, overconsumption and also *receipt of unwants* (e.g. environmental destruction, climate change, pollution, social consequences of algorithms designed to maximise engagement (and minimise engagement with external world)).

I think we need to examine the concept of a "career". Here are various definitions from a cursory search:
- "the job or series of jobs that you do during your working life, especially if you continue to get better jobs and earn more money"
- "doing something regularly for most of your life, especially as your main way of making money"
- "a job for which you are trained and in which it is possible to advance during your working life, so that you get greater responsibility and earn more money"

Overall, "career" seems to represent:
- The set of jobs you have throughout your working life.
- An intentionally crafted trajectory of work toward ever-increasing income and/or purpose (which would perhaps be combined into "increasing prosperity").

Problems arise when we bundle such a range of life motivations into a singular concept. We're effectively collapsing (1) the need to make an income to sustain ourselves materially and (2) the need to feel fulfilled in life, by engaging in work we consider meaningful and in alignment with what we (consciously or subconsciously) perceive to be our purpose.

One of these problems is that we can confuse means-work with meaning-work, and gradually, subconsciously allow means-work to completely dominate our lives, "crowding out" meaning-work. This can look like someone relentlessly pursuing an externally imposed, other-valued idea of "success", such as a "high-flying" career in finance, chasing ever-increasing salary and status at the cost of time and sanity. It can look like someone advancing up to managerial positions by aligning with the motivations of those above them on the corporate ladder, and slowly cognitively dissonancing themselves (distancing themselves from themselves and reality) in thinking that their role is adding rather than subtracting value from the organisation's overall efforts, and society in general [^manager]. It can look like a person gradually acquescing to a life of just-sub-subsatisfactory purpose, 

It can also look like a single mother working 3 jobs, receiving benefits and *still* struggling to afford the bare necessities of life; this mother clearly has no time/energy to pursue things she considers meaningful in life - all her energies are directed towards the means for basic subsistence. 

Here, there are 2 categories of means-dominating-meaning situations we can fall into: (1) mostly self-imposed, or (2) mostly other-imposed. In the case of the money-motivated aspiring top financier, her particular flavour of means-work relates to the means toward the end of material, traditionally, outwardly-acknowledged success; though this idea of success is perhaps in alignment with their own self-made meaning, I suspect she perceives no inherent meaning in the work itself - just in the material outcomes the work can result in. So in this sense, though the work could be considered a "means to her meaning", it's still ultimately means-work, done not for its own sake, but for the sake of another goal, selected (consciously or subconsciously) by herself. This is in contrast to the single mother struggling to make ends meet, who has not had the opportunity to decide on her own meaning and pursue means-work to this end, but has had her particular means-work decided for her, in that she *must* do it in order to subsist in life and provide for her child(ren).

In both cases, the work each person is depleting their finite life energy reserves in service to is a means to an end. With the aspiring financier, the end is decided by her, according to what she considers meaningful. With the struggling mother, the end is decided simply by her existence, and desire for her and her family to exist in non-destitution. The difference between the two ultimately comes down to the extent of material freedom. Now, we could get into the idea of *psychological* freedom and argue that the financier has no more freedom - in this dimension - than the mother, because of subconsciously adopted, socially-conditioned conceptions of success and what a "good life" looks like, which govern the actions taken under her supposed free-will.

But, for the purposes of this discussion, I'll focus only on the material, economic dimension of freedom.

- So, we are working to provide "value" for a *tincy wincy* subset of the population. The majority of us are working to provide value for a minority.
- To identify our finite needs, we need to find what everyone (or at least, a majority) agree is necessary for decent subsistence.
- Of all the things we currently work to provide in the economy, a subset would be considered necessary by the majority.
- This subset is small enough that if we were to each focus on providing within this subset, we could each work *substantially* less.
- With the time gains from this reduction in necessary work, we could focus on what we consider to be meaningful - part of this would be providing in areas where our wants/needs diverge (entertainment, recreation).
- We need to own resources.

- means work is instrumental

I think first, it's important to distinguish between **means work** and **meaning work**.

*Means work* refers to any work we *have* to do to provide things needed for our basic functioning, such as producing food, providing housing, utilities, transport, internet, etc. *Meaning work* refers to work we *want* to do, for no reason other than wanting to do it (though of course, we will probably have reasons for wanting to do it). So, means-work is a means to an end, whereas meaning-work is an end in itself [^hyphen].

Means-work stems from the extrinsic motivation to survive and provide things necessary for for this survival (and we could extend this to include thrival). Meaning-work stems from the intrinsic motivation to do things we feel drawn to do (probably because we sense they will give us a feeling of fulfilment).

In the realm of "thrival", means-work provides things necessary for the meaning-work, which is the thing actually necessary for our thrival (beyond simple survival).

Means-work could be considered work we do as a means of providing for **decent subsistence**, which I would define as a level/standard of living (a lifestyle, or quality of life) which we collectively (subjectively) agree is "decent".

But how do we do we arrive at this collective agreement on a decent lifestyle?
This is where the ideas of **mindware** and cultivating our **digital ecosystem** become particularly useful.

I use the term *mindware* to refer to the hardware and software (basically, any tools/technology) that can help minds to think better, individually (*intrathinking*) and collectively (*interthinking*), and help us to realise (recognise and actualise) Earth's **Mind**, which is the aggregation (and eventually full integration *and* differentiation) of all minds on Earth. But here, I'm getting into more "divergent" terrain which is more my-worldview-specific and is for now, less relevant for these more convergent discussions on how to improve our economy.

So, returning to mindware: this is basically about creating/building/improving the digital infrastructure that enables us to think more effectively, as individuals and a collective - that is, mindware should allow us to simultaneously (1) develop our independent (relatively divergent) thoughts and (2) communicate/express these thoughts with others in order to converge on shared ideals and ideas for progressing toward these ideals.

In developing and improving our mindware, we can better maintain and nurture our *digital ecosystem*: a diverse, heterogenous community of minds, thinking both divergently and convergently. This is in contrast to the current state of the internet, which I would deem a homogenous monoculture (relative to what it used to be and could be). I will interchangeably refer to this digital ecosystem as the *digiscape* (our digital landscape). Within the digiscape exist *digispaces* - digital spaces which belong to a mind or group of minds.

Some key principles for cultivating our digital ecosystem are:
- **Modularity.** Most of our digital infrastructure will probably fall into the category of *mindware modules*, which are "pieces" of mindware that provide different features/functionalities. Each mind can cultivate its own array of functionality (in the form of a unique digital interface - its "portal" into the digiscape) by mixing/matching these modules, where each module is minimally dependent on (ideally, completely independent of) any other modules so that their functionality is mostly additive. It also means that each module is/has its own sandbox - that is, a way to experiment with the module without impinging on (potentially "breaking" or "messing up") functionality from other modules. #tbc
- **Interoperability.** To maximise each mind's freedom in how it interacts with the digiscape, it's important that mindware modules can interact and integrate with each other seamlessly. This allows us to compare modules that provide the same/similar functionality without needing to reconfigure our entire digispace.
- **Divergence mechanisms.** We need mindware modules that allow us to cultivate our own worldview (a representation of everything we think about everything), and then think in terms this unique worldview, so that we can explore thoughts (of our own and other minds) through it, which is what can enable maximal novelty in any thoughts we generate. A more tangible example of this could be a mindware module that has an overview of your worldview which it has inferred from inputting any of your outputs you have labelled as particularly representative of your worldview (e.g. contains terms and concepts you tend to refer to frequently and idiosyncratic grammatical/syntactical habits), and if you choose to turn on your "divergence filter", it parses anything you input (thoughts outputted by others in any format) through this worldview overview, effectively mapping thoughts from others' worldviews onto your own, to enable more efficient/effective processing.
- **Convergence mechanisms.** Just as we have mindware modules for "translating" the thoughts of others into the thoughts more familiar to our own mind (divergence mechanisms), we also need modules for the other direction: parsing our thoughts through the worldviews of other minds, to improve their ability to process, and then integrate, our thoughts into their worldview - i.e., we need mechanisms to converge on commonalities arrived at from our independent divergences.

Part of our digital ecosystem is the digital work that goes into creating its infrastructure. This is about "laying the land", except instead of discovering new terrain, we're *creating* it. We can refer to the act of "digital landscaping" as *digiscaping*.

I think it's useful to consider digiscaping in the context of the superset *mindscaping*, which on top of the digital aspect of our (intra-/inter-)thinking, also includes the beyond-digital material aspects of thinking, such as mindset, mental habits, approaches to thinking independent of technology (e.g. critical thinking), in-person communication and collaboration, etc. And so, it's important to cultivate this kind of psychological and social infrastructure in tandem with the digital infrastructure.

Some thoughts on how the digital ecosystem could look.
We each have our own digispace - our independent thought space where we can input and output thoughts and customise our interface (i.e., choose how others' thoughts are presented to us, and how our thoughts are presented to others). The primary unit of our digiscape is the "thought", appearing in any format thinkable (including multi-format/multi-modal), such as traditional text (articles, essays, tweets, etc.), Instagram-style images plus captions, YouTube-style videos, Spotify-style podcasts, songs, animations, code snippets/web apps, etc. Thoughts, in this context, could also be considered as *posts*, but I prefer the term "thought" because it encompasses media not traditionally labelled as posts (such as songs and computer programs) and I like to tie it to the more theoretical/conceptual approach of cultivating our global Mind and identifying ourselves primarily as *thinkers*, not e.g. "content creators" or "influencers", which the term "posts" has associations with.

You could "deem relevance" of thoughts using a filter module, with which you can specify e.g. certain categories/tags/thinkers you want to be included on (or excluded from) this self-determined/cultivated feed. Relevance could also be assessed using current AI technology - for example, using an LLM to scan for thoughts adjacent to thoughts you've expressed in your own outputs, or that you've specified in a "filter set" (set of thoughts candidate input thoughts must be relevant to to pass through the filter). The beauty of the modular mindware approach is that we can choose what technologies we're comfortable infusing into our digispace. Some of us might feel uncomfortable with the use of LLMs, and so could stick with a more deterministic old-school (but reliable) tagging approach to identifying relevance, or using the level of AI of a basic search engine before advent of LLMs (which I think most wouldn't consider "actual AI" relative to more recent developments). But, for those who wish to embed LLM usage into their digispace, there can be modules that use them, and use different models, in different ways.

Instead of disparate privately owned monolithic platforms we have to choose how to divide our input-output time/energy between, we can I/O in one place. We can design our interfaces around how we prefer to receive/process things. For example, if you like an Instagram-esque feed of images and captions, you could add an "Instagram imitator" module that can transmute all thoughts you've deemed relevant to you into this format. Or, you might like a more article-style input with images embedded throughout. In this case, perhaps you adopt a module that synthesises all relevant inputs into mini-reports you can glance over, and if you notice something you want to hone in on, you can access the original thoughts that fed into the synthesis. Maybe you want a whole jungle of thought formats weaved into a single interface - you have a section for videos, section for images, section for mini-apps, section for "news articles", section for editorial pieces / essays, section for snippets (micro-thoughts, like tweets). Or, maybe you want to separate these into distinct interfaces you can access through different tabs - so, if you feel inclined to watch a video, go onto your personal UTube. If you want to engage with a more "nitty gritty" academic paper/official report, you have a tab for that. #tbc

You can also choose how others can interact with your thoughts - for example, using a module that replicates the like/comment functionality of traditional social media. Ultimately, mindware can give you granular control over specific interaction functionalities, and what thoughts they apply to. Maybe you'd like certain thoughts/posts to be available for commenting, and others to be only available for inputting (no visible interaction). Maybe you'd like to view/monitor the "engagement" with thoughts (or a selection of thoughts), for which you can add an appropriate module to your suite. Or, maybe you don't care / don't want to know that information - you're just happy outputting thoughts and referring to / treating them as a "portfolio" of your work/thinking when you e.g. apply to participate in a project of interest, or reach out to collaborate with other minds.

This is about pursuing full digital (and IPO) autonomy.

Returning to the economy

We could easily identify individuals working/thinking in niches we'd like to delve into. This can help us to self-organise, which is a fundamental aspect of an economy that encourages autonomy.
